People come to Salt Point State Park for all the usual California coast activities: hiking, camping, picnicking, as well as more aquatic pursuits like fishing and scuba-diving. But many visit this park, roughly 100 miles north of San Francisco, simply for the sheer beauty of the unusual rock formations lining the shore. Erosion has carved sedimentary sandstone into wild shapes. Some formations are tall and knobby, while others are large rolling plateaus dotted by tide pools.

Salt Point State Park got its name from the salt that accumulates in small cavities on some of the rock formations. These crevices, called tafoni, form in honeycomb patterns on the surface of the rocks. Seawater carves out the tafoni before settling into them. When dry, the salt crystals can be harvested, which is just what the indigenous Kashaya Pomo people once did, using abalone shells to scrape salt from the rocks.
